How to Invoice as a Photographer

Industries · Free BizDoc · __PUBDATE_EN__

Whether you shoot weddings, products or portraits, your invoice does double duty: it collects payment and sets the terms around deposits, usage and delivery. Here’s how to structure one that protects your time and your images.

Booking deposit and balance

Take a non-refundable booking deposit to hold the date, then invoice the balance before or on delivery of the final images. Make the deposit and balance clear on the invoice, along with the shoot date.

Itemise the shoot and the extras

Separate the shoot fee from editing/retouching, extra hours, prints, albums and travel. If you offer packages, show the package as one line and any add-ons separately so clients see what they chose.

Licensing and delivery

Say whether the client gets personal or commercial usage, and for how long — commercial licensing is usually priced higher. Deliver final high-resolution files after full payment. Apply the right tax for your country (US sales tax varies by state; UK/AU/CA add VAT/GST once registered).

Frequently asked questions

How much deposit should a photographer take?

A booking deposit (often non-refundable) to secure the date is standard — commonly 20–50% of the fee. Invoice the balance before or on delivery of the final images.

Should I license or sell photos on the invoice?

Usually you license usage rather than sell outright. Note the usage (personal or commercial) and duration on the invoice; commercial licensing is typically priced higher.

When should I deliver the final images?

After full payment. State on the invoice that final high-resolution files are delivered once the balance is cleared to avoid chasing payment afterwards.
