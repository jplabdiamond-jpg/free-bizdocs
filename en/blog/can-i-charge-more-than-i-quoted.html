Can I Charge More Than I Quoted?

Quoting · Free BizDoc · Nov 20, 2026

The job turned out bigger than the quote — can you bill for the extra? Sometimes yes, sometimes no. It depends on what you quoted and how you handled the change. Here is how it works.

A firm quote generally binds you

If you gave a firm, fixed quote and the client accepted it, that’s the price for the work you described — you generally can’t just bill more because it took longer. The fixed price is the deal. Charging above it without agreement is where disputes (and bad reviews) come from.

When you can charge more

You can charge more when the work changed: the client added scope, asked for extras, or the job turned out to be a different job than quoted. The test is whether the extra is outside what you originally quoted for — genuinely new work, not just the same work taking longer.

Handle it the right way

The moment you see the job exceeding the quote, stop and tell the client — before doing the extra work. Issue a variation or a new quote for the additional scope and get agreement. Billing a surprise at the end, even for legitimate extra work, feels like a bait-and-switch to the client.

Prevent it next time

Word your quotes to define what’s included and what counts as extra, and add a line that changes to scope will be quoted separately. A tight scope and a change clause turn “can I charge more?” from an awkward argument into a simple, expected process.

Frequently asked questions

Can I charge more than my quote if the job took longer?

Generally no, if you gave a firm fixed quote for the work described &mdash; the same work simply taking longer is your risk. You can charge more when the scope actually changed or extra work was added.

How do I bill for extra work beyond the quote?

Tell the client before doing the extra work, issue a variation or new quote for the additional scope, and get agreement. Don't spring a surprise charge at the end, even for legitimate extras.

How can I avoid quote overruns?

Define clearly what the quote includes and what counts as extra, and add a line that any scope changes will be quoted separately. A tight scope makes extra charges expected rather than disputed.
